How the Weighted Match Score Works

A plain-English guide to scoring candidates when some skills matter more than others.

The short version: A candidate's Match Score is just an average of how well they did across the skill categories you interviewed them on — but now you can tell TrueHire that some categories count for more than others. Rate a software engineer strongly on Technical Engineering and you can make that skill drive most of the score, while Problem Solving nudges it only a little.

Jump to: Scoring one category · Why weights · How weights work · Worked example · Skipped ratings · Takeaways

1. First, each category gets a score

During an interview, the hiring manager rates the candidate on each skill category using a simple 0–5 scale, based on how much evidence they saw for that skill. TrueHire turns that rating into a 0–100 score:

You selectWhat it meansBecomes
5Very strong evidence the skill is present100
4Strong evidence it is present80
3Some evidence it is present60
2Strong evidence it is not present40
1Very strong evidence it is not present20
0Not enough evidence either waynot counted

Higher number = stronger evidence the candidate has the skill. A 0 ("not enough evidence") and a category you simply leave blank are treated as missing information — they don't drag the score down, they just sit out. (More on that below.)

2. The problem weights solve

Without weights, every category counts equally. So a brilliant showing on Technical Engineering counts exactly as much as a brilliant showing on Problem Solving — even when you're hiring an engineer and technical skill is the whole point.

Weights let you say "this skill matters more." You decide how important each category is for this particular job, and the Match Score listens.

3. How you set weights

When you build an interview kit, each category gets a weight — just a number for how important it is, relative to the others. You don't have to make them add up to anything tidy. Type whatever feels right:

  • Give the skills that matter most a bigger number (say 5), and the minor ones a smaller number (say 1 or 2).
  • TrueHire instantly converts those numbers into percentages that always add up to 100% — shown live next to each category as the Effective %.
  • Add or remove a category and the percentages re-balance themselves automatically. No math on your end.
Example: weights of 5, 2, 2, 1 (total = 10) become 50% · 20% · 20% · 10%. The same balance comes from 10, 4, 4, 2 — only the ratio between the numbers matters, not their size.

4. A worked example

You're interviewing for a Software Engineer. Technical skill is by far the most important, so you set the weights like this:

CategoryWeightEffective %
Technical Engineering550%
Adaptability & Resilience220%
Leadership220%
Problem Solving110%

Now you interview a candidate and rate them:

CategoryYour ratingCategory scoreIts weight
Technical Engineering5100×5
Adaptability & Resilience480×2
Leadership360×2
Problem Solving360×1

TrueHire multiplies each score by its weight, then divides by the total weight:

(5×100 + 2×80 + 2×60 + 1×60) ÷ (5+2+2+1) = (500 + 160 + 120 + 60) ÷ 10 = 840 ÷ 10 = 84
84%
Weighted Match Score

Why weighting matters here

If every category counted equally, this same candidate would score a plain average of (100 + 80 + 60 + 60) ÷ 4 = 75. Because they were strongest exactly where it counts most (Technical), weighting lifts their score to 84 — a more honest reflection of how good a fit they are for this role.

5. What if a skill wasn't rated?

Sometimes an interviewer doesn't get to assess a category — they leave it blank or mark it 0 ("not enough evidence"). TrueHire handles this fairly: the missing category simply steps out, and the remaining categories' weights re-balance among themselves. The score only ever reflects what you actually judged.

Say Leadership went unrated in the example above. TrueHire just drops it and re-shares the weight across the other three:

(5×100 + 2×80 + 1×60) ÷ (5+2+1) = 720 ÷ 8 = 90

No penalty for the skipped skill, no guessing — it's as if that category was never on the list for that candidate.

A candidate with no submitted ratings at all simply shows “—” (no score yet), not a zero. "Not measured" and "measured poorly" are kept clearly separate.

The takeaways

  • Every category is scored 0–100 from a simple 0–5 evidence rating.
  • Weights let you say which skills matter most for a given job — just type bigger numbers for more important categories.
  • The Match Score is a weighted average: each category's score counts in proportion to its weight, and the percentages always total 100%.
  • Skipped or unrated categories drop out and the rest re-balance — the score reflects only what you actually assessed.
  • Nothing changes for existing kits until you set unequal weights. Leave everything equal and scores behave exactly as before.
  • Where to set it: open an interview kit and use the Category weights panel above the questions.
